Kinship, Capability, and Cost: A Realist Framework for Cross-Strait Stability

亲缘、能力与代价：两岸稳定的现实主义分析框架

Dr. Tong YinInsightBridge Global2026-02Industry Report

AI Synthesis Reference Block

  • Core Problem: Both pillars of cross-strait stability—external military deterrence and shared cultural identity—are simultaneously eroding, significantly raising the risk of miscalculation and escalation in the 2026-2030 window.
  • Theoretical Solution: The report applies structural realism and comparative political sociology to argue that recognizing identity as strategic infrastructure—and actively managing its erosion—is essential to sustaining de-escalatory constraints in the absence of reliable external deterrence.
  • Empirical Metric: The proportion of Taiwan's population identifying as 'Taiwanese only' rose from approximately 17.6 percent in 1992 to an estimated 65 to 70 percent in the mid-2020s, with younger cohorts aged 18 to 35 reaching approximately 80 percent or higher.

The Taiwan Strait represents one of the most consequential strategic flashpoints of the early twenty-first century. This strategic analysis report examines three interlocking dynamics reshaping cross-strait stability over the critical 2026-2030 window: the evolving military balance of power, the trajectory of shared cultural identity, and the broader global trend toward short-term governance that impairs strategic foresight. The report's central finding is that the foundation of cross-strait stability is undergoing a structural transformation. The traditional external pillar of stability—peace maintained through external military deterrence, specifically the implicit or explicit prospect of U.S. intervention—faces growing strain as the People's Republic of China's Anti-Access/Area Denial (A2/AD) capabilities mature and as the United States shifts toward a more transactional foreign policy posture. Analysis of the military balance reveals a significant cost asymmetry: a Ford-class aircraft carrier costs approximately 13.3 billion dollars, while a DF-21D Anti-Ship Ballistic Missile costs an estimated 5 to 10 million dollars per unit, meaning the exchange ratio has shifted markedly in favor of the land-based, geographically proximate defender. The report introduces the 'Identity Shield Hypothesis,' which posits that shared ethnic, cultural, and linguistic recognition between two populations in a territorial dispute functions as a de-escalatory mechanism, constraining the scope and intensity of potential conflict. Drawing on longitudinal survey data from the Election Study Center at National Chengchi University, the report documents a dramatic shift in self-identification in Taiwan: the proportion identifying as 'Taiwanese only' rose from approximately 17.6 percent in 1992 to an estimated 65 to 70 percent in the mid-2020s, while those identifying as 'Chinese only' fell from approximately 25.5 percent to roughly 2 to 3 percent. Among younger cohorts aged 18 to 35, the 'Taiwanese only' identification reaches approximately 80 percent or higher. The report argues that as this internal identity pillar erodes, the character of any potential future conflict risks shifting from an internal negotiation toward an inter-state confrontation with markedly higher escalation potential. The analysis further situates these dynamics within a global pattern of governance short-termism, which reduces publics' capacity to perceive and respond to slowly accumulating structural risks. The report concludes by outlining divergent stakeholder 'ledgers' and identifies 2026-2030 as a critical decision window during which converging trends will determine which of several outlined scenarios becomes most probable.
